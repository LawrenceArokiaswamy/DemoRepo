What the sources say

"…when he had got hold of her by the prow, would press down the opposite end of the machine which was inside the wall. Then when he had thus by lifting up the ship's prow made her stand upright on her stern … let the chain and hand suddenly drop." (Polybius 8.6)
"…whenever they saw a bit of rope or a stick of timber projecting a little over the wall, 'There it is,' they cried … and turned their backs and fled." (Plutarch, Marcellus 17)
"…every hope of capturing the town at once if one old man of Syracuse were removed." (Polybius 8.7)

What we deliberately leave out or soften

  • Plutarch has ships whirled through mid-air. Polybius, the earlier and more careful source, says the prow was lifted until the ship stood on its stern, then dropped. We show Polybius's version.
  • The machine's dimensions, counterweight and windlass are not described anywhere. That shot is labelled "RECONSTRUCTION".
  • Modern tests (BBC 1999, Discovery 2005) show the idea was feasible, not that it was built this way.
  • A real quinquereme was about 45 m long. Ours is stylised at 30 m to read on a phone.
  • No burning mirrors: that's a later legend.
