# The Claw of Syracuse: research notes

Sources were read in full for this episode (LacusCurtius / Loeb translations):
- **Polybius**, *Histories* 8.3–8.7 (W. R. Paton translation). Polybius wrote in the 2nd century BC, within living memory of the siege, and is the most careful source.
- **Plutarch**, *Life of Marcellus* 14–19 (B. Perrin translation). Plutarch wrote around AD 100, about 300 years later, and is more dramatic.
- **Modern context**:
  - Wikipedia, *Claw of Archimedes*, *Archimedes* and *Siege of Syracuse*.
  - Coates (1995), via Wikipedia *Hellenistic-era warships*, on the size of a quinquereme.
  - Rorres & Harris (2001), a reconstruction paper.
  - C. K. Young, *Centaurus* 46 (2004).

## Key quotations
1. **The claw.** Polybius 8.6.1–4:
   > "...let down an iron hand attached to a chain with which the man who piloted the beam would clutch at the ship, and when he had got hold of her by the prow, would press down the opposite end of the machine which was inside the wall. Then when he had thus by lifting up the ship's prow made her stand upright on her stern, he made fast the opposite end of the machine, and by means of a rope and pulley let the chain and hand suddenly drop from it. The result was that some of the vessels fell on their sides, some entirely capsized, while the greater number, when their prows were thus dropped from a height, went under water and filled, throwing all into confusion."
2. **The night approach.** Polybius 8.5.4–5: Marcellus "was compelled to bring up his ships secretly while it was still night. But when they were close in shore and too near to be struck by the mangonels Archimedes had hit upon another contrivance..."
3. **The plan to get inside the catapults' range.** Plutarch, *Marcellus* 15.5: the Romans hoped that up close the engines "would ... send them flying over their heads, but would be ineffective at close quarters."
4. **Machines hidden until needed.** Polybius 8.5.8: engines "which while invisible at other times, reared themselves when required from inside above the wall, their beams projecting far beyond the battlements."
5. **Marcellus' joke.** Polybius 8.6.6: "Archimedes uses my ships to ladle sea-water into his wine cups..." Plutarch 17.1 has the same joke, plus "this geometrical Briareus."
6. **"There it is!"** Plutarch, *Marcellus* 17.3:
   > "At last the Romans became so fearful that, whenever they saw a bit of rope or a stick of timber projecting a little over the wall, 'There it is,' they cried, 'Archimedes is training some engine upon us,' and turned their backs and fled. Seeing this, Marcellus desisted from all fighting and assault, and thenceforth depended on a long siege."
7. **"One old man."** Polybius 8.7.8–9:
   > "The Romans at least, strong as they were both by sea and land, had every hope of capturing the town at once if one old man of Syracuse were removed; but as long as he was present, they did not venture even to attempt to attack..."
8. **Never again an assault.** Polybius 8.7.5–6: they decided "to resort to any means rather than attempt to take Syracuse by storm ... they never once ventured again upon an assault."
9. **Plutarch's more dramatic version.** Plutarch 15.2–3 has ships "drawn straight up into the air", "whirled hither and thither". **We do NOT show this.** We stage Polybius's version: the prow is lifted until the ship stands on its stern, then dropped.

## Facts and numbers used
- **Siege date:** 213–212 BC. Modern dating of the start varies between 214 and 213 BC. We say "213 BC".
- **Archimedes' age:** born c. 287 BC. This rests on John Tzetzes (12th century AD), who says he lived 75 years and died in 212 BC. So he was "in his seventies", about 74.
- **The fleet:** Marcellus attacked from the sea with 60 quinqueremes (Polybius 8.4.1).
- **Quinquereme size:** a modern estimate (Coates 1995) gives about 45 m long, about 100 tonnes, and about 400 people aboard. **Our ship is stylised at 30 m** so it reads on a phone screen. That is a creative choice.
- **Modern tests:** the claw was tested in the BBC's *Secrets of the Ancients* (1999) and Discovery's *Superweapons of the Ancient World* (2005), which tipped over a model ship. **This shows the idea was feasible, not that the device existed as built.**
- **Burning mirrors:** later legend. Not used.

| Beam reared up over the wall, iron hand on a chain, grabs the prow | Documented (Polybius 8.5.8, 8.6.1–2) |
| Inner end pressed down "like a seesaw" | Documented principle (Polybius 8.6.2) |
| Counterweight box, windlass, A-frame, sizes, number of operators | **Our reconstruction.** On screen: "RECONSTRUCTION" |
| Ship stands upright on its stern, then is dropped; ships capsize or fill | Documented (Polybius 8.6.3–4) |
| Marcellus' joke about ladling sea water | Documented (Polybius 8.6.6; Plutarch 17.1) |
| "There it is!", fleeing from a rope or stick; no more assaults; long siege | Documented (Plutarch 17.3; Polybius 8.7) |
| Archimedes standing on the wall directing the machine | **Dramatisation.** Sources say he designed and built the engines. Plutarch 15.1 says "Archimedes began to ply his engines", but we can't confirm where he stood. |

| Titus, Marcellus' officers, the crew, their faces and reactions | Fictional characters (Marcellus is historical; his look is invented) |
| Ship design (eye, colours, shields, sail) | Stylised reconstruction of a Hellenistic/Roman polyreme |
